Part 2 · What Sygnal does

Four moves, for every handoff

A referral, a follow-up, a scheduled visit or a field task becomes an agreed commitment between two care teams. Sygnal helps make sure it's kept.

1

Facilitate (send)

Help set up the handoff and carry it directly between the two systems. Both sides have a confirmed record of what was sent and received.

The referral is sent and received
2

Track

Follow it against the health system's own rules: accepted, completed, on time.

Accepted within 48 hours?
3

Detect (flag)

When an expected step doesn't happen, the party that was waiting raises a flag. Nobody has to notice by chance.

No confirmation came back
4

Escalate

The flag goes to whoever is accountable, and moves up the line if it stays open.

Supervisor, then programme lead

The outcome Sygnal records is a coordination outcome (completed, closed, still open), never a clinical result.

Part 2 · What Sygnal does

What it delivers, and for whom

Efficacy (continuity of care)

Today a care pathway lives inside one system. Sygnal tells the next care team where the patient is on the pathway and what's due.

Efficiency (less wasted effort)

Fewer repeated registrations, duplicate tests and chased referrals. The receiving provider knows the patient is expected, and why.

Experience (fewer surprises)

Patients don't arrive as strangers. Health workers don't hold follow-ups in their heads; their own systems remind them.

PatientsExpected when they arrive, and less likely to disappear between systems.
Health workersWorklists of who is overdue, so time goes to those at risk.
ProvidersKnow who is coming and why, and whether their referrals were completed.
PayersSee whether care continued across episodes, not just single claims.
Health system leadersSee where patients drop out of care, without a central clinical database.

Part 3 · How it works

The rules we built it around

Edge The systems where care is given: clinics, hospitals, labs, pharmacies and health worker apps.

Centre The shared network services that help those systems work together. The centre never holds patient records and never makes care decisions.

Where data lives

  1. Clinical data stays at the edge
  2. Only basic tracking details reach the centre, and only what the health system allows
  3. Whoever is waiting for the next step raises the alert

When things break

  1. Treatment is never blocked by coordination
  2. Keeps working when shared services are down
  3. Start with one kind of referral and grow

Who controls what

  1. The centre can alert, but never makes care decisions
  2. Clinical decisions stay with clinicians

How it's designed

  1. Reuse before you build
  2. Built for health first, usable in other sectors
  3. Open, with no lock-in
Learn more →Design principles
1 minute. If a proposed feature breaks a principle, the feature is redesigned or rejected. Read the two definitions first; the rest of the deck uses edge and centre in this sense. The two principles to stress: clinical data stays at the edge, and the centre can alert but never makes care decisions. These are the same patterns that made payment and identity infrastructure work at population scale.

Appendix A

Two planes keep clinical content out of the centre

Peer-to-peer plane

Handoffs travel directly between the two systems, signed by both. They carry what the two authorised parties need to act. The shared services are not in this path.

Control plane

Only basic tracking details (metadata) go to and from the shared services: flags, events, policies and advisory signals. Never clinical content.

Learn more →Two planes
1 minute. This is the privacy argument in one picture. The referral itself goes clinic to hospital directly. The network services only see that a handoff was flagged or counted, as the policy allows. If the network services go down, peer-to-peer handoffs carry on.
